01

Company Overview

63 Moons Technologies Ltd (formerly Financial Technologies India Ltd, FTIL, renamed 2016) is a Mumbai-based capital-markets and exchange-technology company — the maker of ODIN (India's most widely used broker trading platform) and DOME, the exchange-matching engine that once powered MCX, NSEL, MCX-SX and others. Founded by Jignesh Shah, it was, in the 2000s, the technology powerhouse behind India's modern exchange ecosystem — until the 2013 NSEL (National Spot Exchange) ₹5,600 Cr payment crisis triggered a cascade of litigation that dismantled its exchange empire and has overhung the stock ever since.

This is therefore not a conventional operating stock — it is a special situation. Three things define it: (1) a decade-long NSEL legal overhang that is finally starting to lift — a ₹1,950 Cr One-Time Settlement, NCLT-approved and now cleared by the MPID Court (July 2026) with asset attachments cancelled; (2) a large treasury/investment book that generates the company's actual profits (interest income alone was ₹131 Cr in FY26) while the core operating business loses money; and (3) a fast-growing cybersecurity subsidiary, 63SATS Cybertech (revenue up 24x to ₹87 Cr, a ₹288 Cr Q1 order book) that acts as the growth "call option." The stock trades below book value (P/B ~0.9x) — a classic deep-value-plus-optionality setup where the debate is unlocking versus value trap.

History & Evolution

Incorporated 1988; became Financial Technologies (India) Ltd (2001). Built ODIN and the DOME exchange engine; promoted MCX (commodities), MCX-SX, NSEL and overseas exchanges — becoming the technology powerhouse of India's modern exchange ecosystem. Peaked near ₹3,048 (2007).
2013 · The NSEL crisis
The National Spot Exchange (NSEL) ₹5,600 Cr payment default erupted — the defining catastrophe. It triggered EOW/CBI/SFIO/ED investigations, civil suits, and regulatory action, dismantling FTIL's exchange empire (forced exits from MCX etc.) and crushing the stock.
2016 · Renamed 63 Moons
Rebranded from FTIL to 63 Moons Technologies — a fresh identity, retaining the core capital-markets-tech IP (ODIN etc.) and a large treasury, while fighting a multi-front legal war.
2017–2024 · Legal war & survival
Years of litigation, merger attempts (a government-ordered FTIL-NSEL merger was struck down by courts — a key legal win), and asset attachments. The company survived on its treasury and legacy tech revenue while the overhang capped the stock.

Cybersecurity subsidiary 63SATS scales 24x to ₹87 Cr; the board approves a ₹1,950 Cr NSEL One-Time Settlement (Mar 2025); NCLT approves it (Nov 2025). New growth engine + settlement path emerge together.

MPID Court (29 Jul 2026) clears the Scheme of Arrangement and cancels the asset attachment to effect the OTS — a landmark de-risking. 63SATS Q1 order book hits ₹288 Cr. Stock rallies sharply.

The Sum-of-the-Parts

Because 63 Moons is a holding-company-like special situation, the right mental model is a sum-of-the-parts, not a single operating business. There are four distinct value buckets:

Why SOTP is the only sensible frame
Each bucket has a completely different character: the treasury is quasi-cash generating steady interest (the actual source of profit); 63SATS is a hyper-growth cybersecurity venture that could be worth a multiple of its revenue if it scales; the legacy exchange-tech business is real IP but lumpy and currently loss-making; and the NSEL settlement is a large cash outflow (₹1,950 Cr) that is partly offset because creditors assign their claims against the original defaulters to 63 Moons (a recovery asset of uncertain value). Blending these into one P/E or one revenue multiple is meaningless — which is exactly why the stock is mispriced and volatile. The investment case rests on whether the market re-rates the sum of these parts as the NSEL overhang clears and 63SATS is valued/monetized.

The key watch-items are order-book conversion, the FY27 revenue/EBITDA delivery, and any eventual value-crystallizing event (fundraise, stake sale, or listing). It's the upside engine — high-potential, high-uncertainty.

The NSEL Overhang, Dissected

The decade-defining issue — and why July 2026 matters
The 2013 NSEL ₹5,600 Cr default is the event that has defined (and depressed) 63 Moons for over a decade — spawning EOW, CBI, SFIO and ED cases, civil suits, and asset attachments, and forcing auditors to issue a qualified opinion every year because the financial impact is genuinely unquantifiable. The path to resolution is a ₹1,950 Cr One-Time Settlement (OTS) with the NSEL Investors Forum (5,682 creditors), approved by the board (Mar 2025) and NCLT (Nov 2025). The breakthrough: on 29 July 2026 the MPID Court allowed the Scheme of Arrangement and cancelled the 2018 asset attachment — the concrete legal step that lets the settlement actually be executed, with further asset releases expected.

The net financial impact is the crux — and it cuts both ways
The OTS is a large cash outflow (₹1,950 Cr) against a treasury of similar order of magnitude — so it materially draws down the asset base that currently underpins the below-book valuation. But two offsets matter: first, in exchange, creditors assign their claims against the actual NSEL defaulters to 63 Moons, giving it a recovery asset (of uncertain but potentially significant value); second, and more importantly, removing a decade of existential legal uncertainty is itself hugely re-rating — a business that can finally be valued on its parts rather than discounted for tail-risk litigation. The honest position: the net cash impact is uncertain (even auditors won't quantify it), but the direction of travel — overhang lifting — is unambiguously positive for the equity story, which is why the stock rallies on each court milestone. This is the single biggest swing factor in either direction.

The single most important thing to understand about the P&L
63 Moons' reported numbers are genuinely confusing and must be read carefully: the consolidated entity posts a net loss (₹22.4 Cr FY26), while the standalone entity posts a large profit (₹174.7 Cr) — but that profit is almost entirely "other income" (interest of ₹131 Cr plus fair-value gains on its treasury), not operating profit. The core operating business loses money (Q4 FY26 had a ₹76 Cr operating loss before other income), and revenue is extremely volatile (₹9.8 Cr to ₹133 Cr across recent quarters — clearly project/one-off recognition, not recurring streams). The takeaway: this is a treasury-plus-optionality entity, not an operating earnings story. Trailing P/E is meaningless (consolidated losses; negative EPS), and even the standalone "profit" is really investment income on the asset base — which is why book value, SOTP and the NSEL catalyst are the only sensible lenses, and headline earnings should be actively ignored as a valuation anchor.

The company's actual profits come from its large treasury/investment book — ₹131 Cr of interest income plus fair-value gains in FY26. It is almost debt-free, and the sheer size of the interest income implies an investment/cash pile that is a substantial fraction of the market cap — which is precisely why the stock trades near or below book value (~₹758/share, P/B ~0.9x).

The treasury is the value floor — but the NSEL settlement is a claim on it
The treasury is the anchor of the deep-value case: a large, liquid asset base generating steady income, with the stock available below the book value of that base. In a clean world, a below-book, cash-rich, debt-free company is a value opportunity. The complication is the ₹1,950 Cr NSEL settlement, which is a direct claim on this treasury — executing the OTS will materially draw it down, so the "book value" backing is not fully free-and-clear until the settlement's net cost (after claim-recovery offsets) is known. This is the crux of the value-versus-trap debate: bulls see a below-book, cash-rich franchise with the overhang lifting; bears see a treasury about to be substantially consumed by a settlement of uncertain net cost. The truth depends on the final net settlement economics and how much value the assigned creditor-claims recover — which is why the July court progress (reducing uncertainty) matters so much to the re-rating.

Management, Promoter & Governance

63 Moons is run day-to-day by MD & CEO S. Rajendran and WTD & CFO Devendra Agrawal, but its identity is inseparable from founder-promoter Jignesh Shah — a brilliant but deeply controversial figure, central to both FTIL's rise and the NSEL saga, and a subject of the ongoing investigations.

Governance and key-man/legal risk are central, not peripheral
For most companies governance is one factor among many; for 63 Moons it is front and centre. The promoter and company remain enmeshed in multiple serious legal proceedings (EOW, CBI, SFIO, ED) that drive the auditor's qualified opinion and the entire overhang. Investors must be clear-eyed: this is a company defined by a contested legal history and a polarizing promoter, where outcomes depend heavily on courts and regulators rather than pure business execution. The counter-view is that 63 Moons has won important legal battles (notably the striking down of the government-ordered FTIL-NSEL merger) and is now negotiating a settlement rather than fighting existentially — a sign of a maturing, resolution-oriented path. Still, anyone investing here is underwriting legal/regulatory and key-man risk to an unusually high degree — it is not a "clean governance" story, and position sizing should reflect that.

The balance sheet is asset-rich and debt-light — total consolidated assets ~₹3,887 Cr, book value ~₹758/share (well above the market price), almost no debt, and a maintained dividend. On paper, a classic below-book, cash-rich value profile.

The metric that decides the thesis: net asset value after the settlement
For a special situation like this, the true test is net asset value (NAV) after the NSEL settlement and any recoveries — not reported earnings. The framework: start with the treasury/investment book + 63SATS value + core-tech value + assigned NSEL claim-recoveries, subtract the ₹1,950 Cr settlement outflow, and compare to the market cap. Today the stock trades below stated book, but stated book doesn't yet fully reflect the settlement's net cost. The key things to track: the final net settlement economics (outflow minus claim recoveries), the pace of asset releases post-July court order, 63SATS's crystallizing value, and whether the operating business narrows its losses. If the net-of-settlement NAV comfortably exceeds the market cap and the overhang clears, the deep-value case is validated; if the settlement consumes most of the treasury with poor claim recovery, the "below book" comfort erodes. NAV-after-settlement is the number that matters.

Bottom line
63 Moons Technologies is one of the Indian market's most distinctive special situations — the former Financial Technologies (FTIL), once the technology backbone of India's exchanges, brought low by the 2013 NSEL crisis and defined ever since by a decade-long legal overhang that is now, finally, visibly lifting (a ₹1,950 Cr One-Time Settlement, NCLT-approved and cleared by the MPID Court in July 2026 with asset attachments cancelled). It is a below-book (P/B ~0.9x), cash-rich, almost debt-free company whose actual profits come from a large treasury (₹131 Cr interest income), while its core operations lose money and revenue is wildly lumpy — so headline earnings are meaningless and even the standalone "profit" is really investment income. The upside is a genuine sum-of-the-parts re-rating: the treasury floor, a fast-growing cybersecurity subsidiary (63SATS, up 24x to ₹87 Cr, ₹288 Cr order book, ₹350 Cr FY27 target) as a real growth call option, assigned NSEL claim-recoveries, and latent exchange-tech IP — all currently discounted for litigation tail-risk that is now receding. Against that stand severe, defining risks: the ₹1,950 Cr settlement has first claim on the treasury (net cost uncertain, auditors won't quantify), the promoter and company remain in serious ongoing legal proceedings, the core business is loss-making, and the whole thesis hinges on courts as much as execution. The honest framing: a genuine high-risk, high-reward deep-value-plus-optionality play at an inflection — value it strictly on SOTP / NAV-after-settlement and P/B (never earnings), treat 63SATS as upside rather than base case, make NSEL-resolution progress the deciding gate, and size it as the litigation-underwriting bet it is. The July 2026 court breakthrough is a real positive turn; the net economics are what remain to be proven.

The metric that decides the thesis: net asset value after settlement & recoveries
The whole valuation reduces to one uncertain number — the treasury/NAV that survives the NSEL settlement. Bulls compute: treasury + 63SATS + core-tech + assigned claim-recoveries − ₹1,950 Cr settlement, and find a figure comfortably above the market cap (hence below-book = opportunity). Bears note that ₹1,950 Cr is a very large claim on that treasury and claim-recoveries are speculative, so the net book could be far lower than the stated ₹758/share. The truth is genuinely unknown until the settlement executes and recoveries play out — which is why the stock re-rates on each step that reduces this uncertainty.
