Chapter 1 · Foundations

The capital markets value chain

Foundation

Who does what to a trade, and why one system across all of them matters.

A bank's markets and treasury business buys and sells financial instruments. Some trades serve clients: a corporate hedging its USD import payments, or a mutual fund lending bonds. Some manage the bank's own balance sheet: treasury funding the bank, or hedging the interest rate risk of its loan book. Some are market-making, where the bank quotes prices and holds the resulting risk for a short time.

Every trade passes through the same functions, whatever the product:

Why "front to back to risk" matters

Historically, banks ran a separate system per function and per asset class: an FX system, a derivatives system, a settlement system, a risk engine, a general ledger. Each held its own copy of the trade. Every copy had to be reconciled, and each reconciliation was a source of breaks, delays and operational risk. An integrated platform keeps one trade record that every function reads. Murex describes MX.3 as a "front-to-back-to-risk" platform for this reason.

The benefit is consistency: the P&L the trader sees, the sensitivities risk reports and the accounting entries all come from the same trade and, if configured that way, the same market data. The cost is coupling: a change to shared static data, a curve or a trade classification affects every function at once. Most of the expert skill in running such a platform is managing that coupling.

The layers in plain terms

Presentation. Users work in the desktop client or a browser. Other systems reach MX.3 through APIs. Nothing is calculated here; slowness at this layer is usually a symptom of work below.

Business engines. These do the work: price a trade, move it through a workflow, generate an accounting entry, compute XVA, check a limit. They are shared: the same pricing engine serves the trader's screen, the EOD revaluation and the risk run. This is how MX.3 keeps FO and risk numbers consistent.

Orchestration. Large jobs such as a VaR run over thousands of scenarios, or collateral calls across thousands of agreements, are split into pieces and spread across many servers. Murex cites processing 20,000 collateral agreements in 10 minutes and more than 1,000 trades per second. Grid computing, including GPUs for exotic pricing, lives here. Big-data components such as Apache Spark and Storm, and an in-memory data grid, are part of this layer's published technology.

Technical services. Log-in, permissions, messaging between services, caching and monitoring. SAML handles single sign-on; OpenTelemetry exposes metrics and traces to monitoring tools.

Data. Two logical databases: the financial database, which holds trades, static data, market data and results, and the datamart database, which holds reporting tables filled in batch. Keeping them separate means heavy reporting does not slow down trading.

Deployment

The layers can run on bare metal, virtual machines or containers. Murex publishes support for Kubernetes and containers, elastic compute for market risk and reporting, and cloud deployment on certified hyperscalers. On MXSaaS, Murex runs the infrastructure and upgrades; the bank keeps its configuration and business processes.

Environments

Every implementation runs several copies of the platform. The names vary, but a typical chain is: development (configuration and interface build), SIT (system integration testing), UAT (business acceptance), pre-production or parallel run (production-like data and volumes), production, and often a DR (disaster recovery) copy. Configuration is built in development and promoted through the chain. Direct changes in production are an audit finding in any well-run bank.

In MX.3

  • Services are started and managed as a group; practitioners talk about launchers hosting services, and a file server and XML server among the core services. Your technical team will have a service map; ask for it.
  • Batch work runs as processing scripts: named sequences of tasks such as market data import, revaluation, feeder runs and date roll, scheduled by an enterprise scheduler (Control-M, Autosys or similar) or by MX.3 itself.
  • Access is controlled by user groups and rights: which screens, portfolios, entities and actions a person can use. Segregation of duties (a trader cannot validate their own trade) is enforced here.

Expert notes

  • When a screen is slow, ask which engine it calls. A simulation over a large portfolio is a calculation problem; a slow trade search is a database problem; a frozen screen during EOD may be contention with the batch.
  • Keep a diagram of which environment has which data date. Most UAT defects that "cannot be reproduced" come from different market data or static data between environments.
  • On SaaS, you give up control of upgrade timing. Build automated regression tests (chapter 29) before you need them.

The data model

Practitioner

Static data, market data, trade data and results. This is the chapter to know best, because most breaks start in the data.

Think of MX.3 data in four families. Static data describes the world: who, what, where, how. Market data describes prices on a date. Trade data describes what the bank did. Results are what the platform calculated from the other three.

Static data

Entities, counterparties, portfolios, instruments, conventions, calendars, settlement instructions, agreements. Changes rarely; affects everything.

Market data

Rates, curves, volatilities, spreads, prices, fixings. Changes constantly; stored by date and by market data set.

Trade data

Contracts, their legs and cash flows, events, versions and statuses. Grows every day.

Results

Valuations, sensitivities, P&L, accounting entries, settlement instructions, datamart tables. Recomputed daily.

Market data in depth

Market data has three properties that matter: what it is (a quote, a curve, a surface), when (live, or a closing snapshot for a date), and which set it belongs to. MX.3 lets a bank keep several market data sets, for example a front-office set updated live, an official closing set for P&L and accounting, and a risk set. Each consumer (screen, batch step, report) is configured to read a particular set.

Raw quotes are rarely used directly. They are inputs to calibrated objects: a curve built from deposit, futures and swap quotes (chapter 7), a volatility surface fitted to option quotes, a credit curve built from CDS spreads. When a trader says "the curve", they usually mean the calibrated object, not the quotes.

Trade data in depth

A trade in MX.3 has several layers:

  • Contract and trade. The economic deal, with an identifier that stays constant, and versions each time it is changed.
  • Legs. A swap has two legs; a cross-currency swap two legs in different currencies; a package may contain several linked contracts.
  • Schedules and cash flows. Generated from the conventions: accrual start and end, fixing date, payment date, amount (known or projected).
  • Events. Everything after booking: amendment, fixing, exercise, termination, novation. Each event has its own date and effect.
  • Statuses. Where the trade and each event sit in their workflows (chapter 18).
  • Audit trail. Who did what and when. This is your best friend when investigating.

Three dates on every trade

Each trade carries a trade date (when the deal was agreed), an entry or input date (when it was booked in the system) and a value or start date (when it takes economic effect). Events have similar dates. The gap between trade date and entry date is the source of most "back-dated" P&L and accounting surprises.

In MX.3

  • A new trade is almost always booked from a template (generator or instrument definition), so conventions come from static data, not from the trader's typing. When a cash flow looks wrong, look at the template first.
  • Typology and portfolio together decide much of the downstream processing. Many banks treat a new typology as a controlled change that finance, operations and risk must approve.
  • Static data is often mastered elsewhere (a counterparty golden source, a securities master) and fed into MX.3. Know which fields MX.3 owns and which it only receives, so you fix problems at the source.

Expert notes

  • When investigating anything, write down four things first: the trade ID and version, the event, the date, and the market data set. Half of all investigations go wrong because two people are looking at different versions or dates.
  • Static data changes have a delayed blast radius: a calendar change today affects trades that fix next month. Include forward-looking impact checks in static data change control.

Chapter 6 · Front office

Pricing foundations

Foundation

Time value, conventions and discounting: the arithmetic under every valuation.

Every price in MX.3 comes from one idea: a cash flow in the future is worth less than the same cash today, and the difference is set by interest rates. To value any instrument, the platform does three things: works out each future cash flow, works out when it is paid, and discounts it to today. The rest is detail, but the detail is where the breaks come from.

Day count conventions

Interest accrues over a period measured in years, and there are several ways to count the fraction:

  • Act/365 (Fixed): actual days divided by 365. Used for INR, GBP and many money-market conventions.
  • Act/360: actual days divided by 360. Used for USD and EUR money markets and SOFR, €STR.
  • 30/360: each month counted as 30 days. Common for bond coupons, including Indian government securities.
  • Act/Act: actual days over actual days in the period or year. Used for many government bonds (for example US Treasuries).

Worked example: day count

Interest on ₹10 crore at 6.50% for 91 days:

Act/365: 10,00,00,000 × 6.50% × 91/365 = ₹16,20,548
Act/360: 10,00,00,000 × 6.50% × 91/360 = ₹16,43,056

A difference of ₹22,508 on one deposit. A wrong day count on a template affects every trade booked from it, which is why conventions belong in controlled static data.

Business-day conventions and calendars

When a date falls on a holiday it moves. Following moves to the next business day; modified following does the same unless that crosses into the next month, in which case it moves back; preceding moves back. Which holidays count depends on the calendar: a USD/INR trade needs Mumbai and New York holidays, and FX settlement usually needs both currencies' calendars plus USD.

Two more conventions matter for swaps. Adjusted or unadjusted accrual: whether the interest period ends on the moved date or the original one. And payment lag: how many business days after the period end the payment is made (common for RFR swaps).

Compounding and discount factors

A discount factor DF(t) is the value today of 1 unit paid at time t. With a zero rate z quoted with annual compounding, DF(t) = 1 / (1 + z)t. With continuous compounding, DF(t) = e−zt. The platform stores curves in whatever form it likes internally; what matters is that each consumer uses the same convention when converting. Mixing compounding conventions is a classic cause of small, persistent P&L differences.

Present value

The present value of an instrument is the sum of its expected cash flows, each multiplied by its discount factor. For instruments with optionality (caps, swaptions, FX options), "expected" means the average over possible market outcomes under a pricing model. Linear products need only curves; options also need volatilities.

In MX.3

Conventions live in the templates and calendars described in chapter 4. The pricing library applies them when generating the schedule, projecting floating rates and discounting. A trader overriding a convention on one trade creates an exception that every downstream team inherits; many banks restrict which fields a trader may override.

Chapter 7 · Front office

Curves: construction and use

Practitioner

How a yield curve is built from market quotes, why modern banks use several curves, and what that means for risk.

A curve turns a handful of market quotes into a discount factor for every date. Building one is called bootstrapping or calibration: start with the shortest instrument, solve for its discount factor, then use it to solve the next, and so on. The curve reprices every input instrument exactly.

Worked example: bootstrapping from par swap rates

Interpolation

Between the quoted tenors the platform interpolates. Common choices are linear on zero rates, linear on log discount factors (which gives piecewise-flat forward rates), and smooth methods such as monotone convex or cubic splines. The choice matters: flat-forward curves produce jumpy forwards but local risk; smooth curves produce smooth forwards but spread risk across tenors. Changing the interpolation method changes valuations and risk buckets, so it is a controlled change.

Multi-curve: why one curve is not enough

Before 2008, banks used one curve per currency to both project floating rates and discount cash flows. The crisis showed that lending for three months is riskier than rolling overnight lending, so 3-month and 6-month rates carry a spread over overnight rates. Banks moved to multi-curve frameworks:

  • A discounting curve based on the rate paid on collateral, usually the overnight rate (OIS curve), because a collateralised derivative is effectively funded at that rate.
  • Projection curves per index and tenor, used to forecast floating rates, each built as a spread over the discounting curve using basis swap quotes.
  • CSA-specific discounting: if a CSA allows collateral in several currencies, the counterparty will post the cheapest one, and the discounting curve reflects that choice.
  • Cross-currency curves: discounting USD cash flows under an INR CSA (or the reverse) needs cross-currency basis, taken from FX forwards and cross-currency swaps.

With the end of LIBOR, most major currencies now reference overnight risk-free rates directly (SOFR, SONIA, €STR, TONA), which simplifies projection for new trades. In India, the MIBOR OIS curve is the main INR curve; Modified MIFOR (MMIFOR) replaced MIFOR for USD/INR-linked products.

Special features

Turn-of-year and month-end effects: overnight rates can jump on balance-sheet dates, so curves often include explicit jumps. Central bank meeting dates: short-end overnight curves may be built with steps on policy meeting dates. Futures convexity: rates implied from futures need a convexity adjustment before they enter a curve.

Caps, floors and swaptions

Bonds

A bond pays fixed coupons and returns its face value at maturity. It trades on a clean price (without accrued interest) and settles on the dirty price (clean plus accrued). FX and money markets

Practitioner

Spot, forwards, swaps, NDFs and options: the highest-volume products in most treasuries.

FX is where volume lives: Murex cites handling about 25% of global FX trading on its platform and processing 35 million FX cash trades a day in benchmarks. A spot trade settles in two business days (T+2) for most pairs, one day for USD/CAD and some others. A forward (outright) settles later at a rate set today. An FX swap combines a near leg and an opposite far leg, and is mostly a funding instrument. An NDF (non-deliverable forward) settles only the difference in a convertible currency, for currencies with capital controls.

Forward rates and covered interest parity

The forward rate is not a forecast. It is set by the interest rates in the two currencies, so that borrowing in one, converting, and investing in the other gives no free profit.

Worked example: a 3-month USD/INR forward

Fictional spot 88.00, INR rate 6.50% (Act/365), USD rate 4.00% (Act/360), 91 days.

F = S × (1 + rINR × 91/365) / (1 + rUSD × 91/360) = 88.5309
Forward points = 53.09 paise; annualised forward premium ≈ 2.42%

The higher-yielding currency (INR) trades at a forward discount, so USD/INR forwards sit above spot. In practice traders quote the points, and actual forward premia also reflect onshore supply and demand and cross-currency basis.

Value at Risk and expected shortfall

VaR answers: over a horizon (usually 1 day, scaled to 10 for capital), what loss will not be exceeded with a given confidence (99% or 95%)? Expected shortfall (ES) answers: if we are in the worst tail, what is the average loss? ES captures the shape of the tail, which VaR ignores, and is the measure FRTB uses.

Three methods: historical simulation (apply each of the last N days' market moves to today's portfolio; the most common in banks and the approach Murex highlights), parametric (assume a distribution and use sensitivities), and Monte Carlo (simulate many scenarios from a model). Scenarios can be applied by full revaluation (reprice every trade; accurate but heavy) or sensitivity-based approximation (fast but misses non-linearity).

Worked example: historical VaR with 20 scenarios

Portfolio P&L (₹ lakh) when each of 20 fictional historical days' moves is applied to today's positions:

-4.1, +1.2, +0.8, -1.5, +2.3, -0.4, -6.8, +1.9, +0.2, -2.2, +3.1, -0.9, -3.4, +0.6, +1.4, -1.1, +2.7, -5.2, +0.3, -0.7

Sorted, the worst four are -6.8, -5.2, -4.1, -3.4. With 20 scenarios, the 95% tail holds one scenario (5% of 20). Conventions differ on whether VaR is the worst loss or the next one; taking the second-worst loss:

95% VaR = ₹5.2 lakh    95% ES (average of the two worst) = ₹6.0 lakh

Real banks use 250 to 500 or more days, so a 99% VaR sits between the 2nd and 3rd worst of 250. Document the exact convention: it changes results and backtesting.

Stress testing

Stress tests apply extreme but plausible moves: historical (replay 2008, the 2013 taper tantrum, March 2020) or hypothetical (a 200 bp parallel shift, INR depreciating 10%). Murex publishes support for both. Regulators also set supervisory scenarios. Stress results feed limits and capital planning.

Backtesting

Compare each day's VaR with the actual and hypothetical P&L. Too many days where losses exceed VaR (exceptions) means the model underestimates risk; under Basel rules, more than four exceptions at 99% over 250 days starts to increase the capital multiplier.

FRTB: the Fundamental Review of the Trading Book

Expert

The Basel market risk capital framework, and why it forces front office and risk onto the same data.

FRTB (Basel Committee standard, finalised in 2019) replaces the older VaR-based market risk capital rules. Banks compute capital under a standardised approach (FRTB-SA), which every bank must calculate, and may use the internal models approach (FRTB-IMA) desk by desk if the desk passes strict tests. Implementation dates differ by jurisdiction and several have been delayed; check the dates and approach that apply to your bank, including RBI's current market risk capital directions for Indian banks.

Standardised approach: three parts

  • Sensitivities-based method (SBM): delta, vega and curvature charges for seven risk classes (general interest rate, credit spread in three types, equity, commodity, FX). Sensitivities are weighted by prescribed risk weights, then aggregated with prescribed correlations within and across buckets, under three correlation scenarios (low, medium, high); the highest total is taken.
  • Default risk charge (DRC): for jump-to-default risk on credit and equity positions.
  • Residual risk add-on (RRAO): a simple charge on gross notional for exotic payoffs (1%) and other residual risks (0.1%).

Internal models approach

  • Expected shortfall at 97.5%, calibrated to a stressed period, with liquidity horizons of 10 to 120 days depending on the risk factor.
  • Non-modellable risk factors (NMRF): factors without enough real price observations are capitalised separately through stressed scenarios, often a large charge.
  • Backtesting at desk level (99% and 97.5% VaR over 250 days).
  • P&L attribution (PLA) test: compares each desk's hypothetical P&L (front-office pricing) with its risk-theoretical P&L (risk model) using the Spearman correlation and the Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance. A desk in the red zone must use the standardised approach.

PLA is why FRTB matters for platform design: any difference between the front-office valuation and the risk model's view (different curves, different interpolation, missing risk factors, proxies) shows up as a failed test and more capital.

Chapter 14 · Risk

Counterparty credit risk, XVA and SA-CCR

Expert

What the bank loses if a counterparty defaults, how that is priced, and how much capital it needs.

Types of credit exposure

  • Issuer risk: holding a bond or loan; lose it if the issuer defaults.
  • Settlement risk: paying your side of an exchange before receiving theirs. FX is the classic case; CLS and PVP settlement remove it.
  • Pre-settlement (counterparty) risk: a derivative has positive value to you and the counterparty defaults before it matures. The exposure moves with markets.

Measuring pre-settlement exposure

Today's exposure is the positive MTM. Future exposure is uncertain, so it is simulated: generate thousands of market paths (Monte Carlo), revalue the netting set on each path at future dates, apply netting and collateral, and take statistics. Expected exposure (EE) is the average positive exposure at each date; potential future exposure (PFE) is a high percentile (say 95% or 97.5%), used for credit limits. Netting sets (trades under one ISDA with enforceable netting) and collateral agreements are critical static data here.

XVA: valuation adjustments

Supervisory duration SD = (e−0.05×0 − e−0.05×3) / 0.05 = 2.7858
Adjusted notional = 100 crore × 2.7858 = ₹2,78,58,40,471
Add-on = supervisory factor 0.5% × adjusted notional × delta (+1) × maturity factor (1) = ₹1,39,29,202

Chapter 16 · Risk and operations

Collateral and margin

Practitioner

CSAs, variation and initial margin, the uncleared margin rules and SIMM, and CCP margin.

Collateral reduces counterparty risk. Under a CSA (credit support annex to the ISDA master agreement), parties exchange collateral as the MTM moves. The key terms are the threshold (exposure allowed before collateral is due), the minimum transfer amount (MTA), eligible collateral and its haircuts, rounding, the valuation frequency and the interest paid on cash collateral.

Worked example: a variation margin call

Exposure to the counterparty (netting-set MTM): ₹4,35,00,000
Threshold: ₹1,00,00,000   MTA: ₹25,00,000   Rounding: down to ₹10 lakh
Collateral required = MTM − threshold = ₹3,35,00,000
Already held: ₹2,80,00,000
Shortfall = ₹55,00,000, above the MTA, so a call is due
After rounding: call for ₹50,00,000

Variation margin and initial margin

Variation margin (VM) covers the current MTM and moves daily. Initial margin (IM) covers the potential move during the time needed to close out after a default, and is held separately (segregated with a custodian for uncleared trades) so neither party can reuse it.

Uncleared margin rules (UMR)

After 2008, regulators required margin on derivatives that are not centrally cleared. VM applies broadly. IM was phased in by size between 2016 and 2022, down to groups with more than €8 billion of average notional; IM only has to be exchanged above a €50 million threshold per group relationship. The industry calculates IM with ISDA SIMM, a sensitivity-based model: delta, vega and curvature by risk class, weighted and correlated like FRTB-SA but with its own parameters, which ISDA recalibrates regularly. A schedule-based method (percentage of notional) is the simpler, usually more expensive alternative. India has its own RBI directions on margining non-centrally cleared derivatives.

Cleared derivatives

For trades cleared through a CCP (LCH, CME, CCIL in India), the CCP calls VM and IM using its own models. The bank's job is to reconcile the CCP's calls with its own projections and fund them.

Collateral optimisation

When several assets are eligible, a bank posts the cheapest to deliver (after haircuts and funding costs) and keeps high-quality assets for liquidity. Optimisation runs across all agreements and the inventory of available assets.

Chapter 17 · Treasury

Treasury, liquidity and ALM

Expert

Funding, liquidity ratios, transfer pricing and interest rate risk in the banking book.

Treasury manages the bank's own balance sheet: it raises funding (deposits, CDs, CP, repo, bonds), invests surplus liquidity, holds a buffer of liquid assets, and hedges the interest rate and currency risk of the banking book. Its view of the platform centres on cash flows: contractual cash flows from every instrument, plus behavioural estimates for items like savings deposits that have no fixed maturity.

Liquidity measures

The liquidity ladder projects net cash flows by time bucket. The Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR) requires high-quality liquid assets (HQLA) at least equal to net cash outflows over a 30-day stress. The Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR) requires stable funding at least equal to the stable funding needed over one year. Both must be at least 100%.

Worked example: LCR

Fictional figures (₹ crore). Level 1 HQLA 500 (no haircut), Level 2A 100 (15% haircut), Level 2B 40 (50% haircut). Stressed outflows 800, inflows 300 (inflows are capped at 75% of outflows).

HQLA = 500 + 100 × 0.85 + 40 × 0.50 = ₹605 cr
Net outflows = 800 − min(300, 0.75 × 800) = ₹500 cr
LCR = 605 / 500 = 121.0%

Level 2 assets are also capped at 40% of HQLA, and Level 2B at 15%; neither cap binds here.

Funds transfer pricing (FTP)

FTP charges each business for the funding it uses and credits it for the funding it provides, at an internal curve (base rate plus liquidity premium). It moves interest rate and liquidity risk from the businesses to treasury, where it is managed centrally. For derivatives, FVA is the equivalent of FTP.

Interest rate risk in the banking book (IRRBB)

Two views: economic value of equity (EVE), the change in the present value of all banking-book cash flows under rate shocks, and net interest income (NII), the change in earnings over a horizon. Basel sets six standard shock scenarios (parallel up and down, steepener, flattener, short rates up and down) and an outlier test that flags a bank whose EVE loss exceeds 15% of Tier 1 capital. Repricing gap reports show how much of the balance sheet reprices in each bucket.

Chapter 23 · Finance

IFRS 9, Ind AS 109 and the RBI framework

Expert

Classification, measurement and the effective interest method, with the Indian overlay.

Classification under IFRS 9

Financial assets are classified by two tests: the business model (hold to collect cash flows, hold to collect and sell, or other) and the SPPI test (are the cash flows solely payments of principal and interest?). The result is one of three categories:

  • Amortised cost: hold to collect, and SPPI. Interest by the effective interest method; impairment (expected credit loss) applies.
  • Fair value through OCI (FVOCI): hold to collect and sell, and SPPI. Fair value changes go to other comprehensive income; interest and impairment through P&L.
  • Fair value through P&L (FVTPL): everything else, including all derivatives not in hedge relationships and all trading assets.

Ind AS 109 in India is substantially converged with IFRS 9. Separately, Indian banks follow the RBI's investment classification and valuation framework, revised with effect from April 2024: held to maturity (HTM), available for sale (AFS) and fair value through P&L (FVTPL, with held for trading as a subset). Where both apply, the platform needs two sets of rules.

Effective interest rate

For amortised-cost assets bought at a discount or premium, or with fees, interest income is recognised at the effective interest rate (EIR): the rate that discounts the expected cash flows to the initial carrying amount. The discount or premium is amortised over the life.

Worked example: EIR amortisation

A 3-year bond with a 6% annual coupon, face 100, bought at 97.30 and held at amortised cost. The EIR that equates the cash flows to 97.30 is 7.0294%.

IFRS 9 impairment uses a three-stage model: 12-month expected loss for performing assets (stage 1), lifetime expected loss after a significant increase in credit risk (stage 2), and lifetime loss with interest on the net amount for credit-impaired assets (stage 3). Chapter 24 · Finance

Hedge accounting

Expert

Removing the accounting mismatch between a hedge at fair value and a hedged item that is not.

A bank hedges a fixed-rate bond (at amortised cost) with a pay-fixed swap (at FVTPL). Economically the risk is hedged, but the accounts show the swap's fair value swings in P&L while the bond's opposite change is invisible. Hedge accounting fixes the mismatch, if the bank documents and tests the relationship.

Three types

  • Fair value hedge: hedge changes in fair value of an asset or liability. The hedged item is adjusted for the hedged risk through P&L, offsetting the derivative.
  • Cash flow hedge: hedge variability in future cash flows (a floating-rate loan, a forecast FX purchase). The effective part of the derivative's change goes to a cash flow hedge reserve in equity, and is released to P&L when the hedged cash flow hits.
  • Net investment hedge: hedge the FX exposure of a foreign subsidiary.

Requirements

Formal designation and documentation at inception; an economic relationship between hedge and hedged item; credit risk not dominating; and a hedge ratio consistent with risk management. IFRS 9 removed IAS 39's 80–125% bright-line effectiveness test, but banks still measure effectiveness and book ineffectiveness in P&L. Many banks still apply IAS 39 for macro (portfolio) hedges of interest rate risk, which IFRS 9 allows.

Worked example: dollar-offset effectiveness

Change in fair value of the hedged bond due to rates: −4.10
Change in fair value of the swap: +3.90
Offset ratio = 3.90 / 4.10 = 95.1%

Highly effective, within the old 80–125% range. The ineffective part is recognised in P&L (for a fair value hedge, the net −0.20).

In MX.3

Murex describes a hedge accounting module covering micro and macro hedges, "smart designation", effectiveness measurement and dashboard monitoring, compliant with IFRS 9. It is often licensed separately; check whether your bank uses it or runs hedge accounting elsewhere.

Chapter 25 · Finance

Product control: IPV, reserves and P&L sign-off

Expert

Making sure the reported P&L and fair values are true.

Daily P&L sign-off

Each morning product control produces the official P&L for the previous day, explains it (chapter 12), resolves differences with the trader's estimate, and gets sign-off. Murex lists P&L attribution, validation and trader sign-off among its finance features.

Independent price verification (IPV)

Traders mark their own closing curves and volatilities, which is a conflict of interest. IPV compares those marks with independent sources (broker quotes, consensus services, exchange prices) at least monthly, and books adjustments where the difference exceeds tolerance.

Fair value hierarchy and reserves

IFRS 13 ranks fair values: Level 1 (quoted prices for identical instruments), Level 2 (models with observable inputs), Level 3 (significant unobservable inputs). Level 3 positions need extra disclosure and often reserves. Reserves or valuation adjustments cover bid-offer (closing out at mid is not possible), model risk, concentration, and day-one profit on Level 3 trades, which cannot be recognised immediately.

XVA in the accounts

IFRS 13 fair value includes counterparty and own credit risk, so CVA and DVA (and in practice FVA at many banks) are booked. Murex publishes deal-by-deal CVA/FVA attribution for finance, compliant with IFRS 13 and ASC 820.

Prudent valuation

European regulation adds prudent valuation adjustments (AVAs), deducted from capital, reflecting valuation uncertainty. Other regulators have similar expectations.

Expert notes

  • Keep the IPV market data separate from the front-office set, and store the source of every IPV price. Auditors will ask.
  • When P&L differs between front office and product control, the cause is almost always one of: market data set, cut-off time, trade population, or a reserve booked by one side only.

Month-end and year-end

Month-end adds accounting closes, regulatory snapshots and extra reports, so the batch runs longer and has less slack. Year-end adds more, and often a freeze on changes. Plan platform changes around these dates.

Reruns

When a step fails or produced wrong results, it may be rerun after a fix. Every rerun must answer: which steps depend on it and must also rerun, which outputs have already been sent downstream (and must be recalled or corrected), and who approves.

  • A forced run with stale data is almost always worse than a late correct run. Agree the escalation rule in advance with product control and risk.
  • Watch batch duration trends. A step creeping from 20 to 40 minutes over six months will break the SLA at month-end first.

Chapter 27 · Reporting

Datamart and reporting

Practitioner

How data leaves MX.3 for reports, regulators and downstream systems.

Operational screens show live data for one user. Reports need consistent snapshots across the whole bank. MX.3 separates the two with its datamart: a reporting database filled by batch jobs.

Financial databaseTrades, market data, results
FeedersBatch jobs that extract and calculate
Datamart tablesStored by date and run
ExtractionsQueries that shape report output
ConsumersReports, files, data warehouse, regulators

Key ideas

  • Feeders populate reporting tables: trade details, cash flows, sensitivities, P&L, accounting entries. They are grouped into batches that run in the EOD (and sometimes intraday). Each run is stamped with a reference or date, so a report can say exactly which snapshot it shows.
  • Extractions read those tables, filter and format them into files or reports.
  • Dynamic tables and viewers in the GUI serve ad-hoc questions on live data, and are often the quickest way to investigate a single trade.
  • The datamart can run on its own database, so heavy reporting does not slow trading.

Why reports disagree with screens

The screen is live; the report is a snapshot. Common causes of differences: the feeder ran before a late amendment; the report filters a different portfolio node or entity; the report uses a different market data set; or the feeder failed and the report read the previous run.

Expert notes

  • Datamart developers are among the most in-demand Murex technical roles. The skills are SQL, understanding MX.3's data model, and performance tuning of feeders.
  • Never let consumers read the financial database directly for production reports. It couples them to internal tables that change in upgrades.
  • Version every report definition and test it in upgrades; a changed table in the new version breaks silently otherwise.

Chapter 29 · Delivery

Upgrades, testing and change management

Expert

Keeping a live platform current without breaking it.

Upgrades

MX.3 releases bring new features, regulatory changes and fixes. Staying too far behind loses support and makes the eventual jump harder. An upgrade needs a copy of production configuration and data in a test environment, regression testing of every critical process (EOD, P&L, risk, accounting, reports, interfaces), a comparison of results between old and new versions, and a cut-over plan. On MXSaaS, Murex drives the timing, so regression testing must be ready and fast.

Test automation

Murex offers MxTest, a front-to-back test automation tool: it can insert trades, apply events, run processing scripts and compare results against a baseline. Murex has also publicised clients adopting continuous integration and delivery (CI/CD) for MX.3 configuration. Automated non-regression is what makes frequent upgrades survivable.

Configuration change management

Configuration (templates, workflows, rules, reports) is effectively code. Treat it that way: version it, package changes, promote through environments, test, and approve. Keep a record of who changed what, why, and with whose approval. Many audit findings in MX.3 banks are about uncontrolled configuration changes.

Archiving and performance

Matured trades and old results accumulate. Archiving and purging policies move them out of live processing while keeping them available for audit and regulatory retention. Without them, EOD and screens slow down year after year.

Expert notes

  • Build a "golden" regression portfolio: a set of trades that covers every product, event and workflow path you support, with known expected results. It pays for itself at the first upgrade.
  • Compare numbers between versions at trade level, not portfolio level. Offsetting differences hide real changes.

Troubleshooting playbook

Expert

A method for any break, and the twenty problems you will see most.

A method that works for any break

  1. Pin the facts. Trade ID and version, event, date, entity, portfolio, market data set, environment. Most wasted hours come from two people looking at different versions.
  2. Find the first wrong number. Follow the chain upstream (report → datamart → result → trade → static or market data) until the data is right; the break is just after that point.
  3. Ask what changed. Trades, market data, static data, configuration, version. The audit trail and change log answer most questions.
  4. Size the blast radius. One trade or all trades from one template? One day or since a change?
  5. Fix at the source, then correct the outputs. Re-running without fixing the source brings the break back tomorrow.
  6. Prevent recurrence. A control, a validation rule, or a test case in the regression pack.
