01

Company Overview

Palantir Technologies (NASDAQ: PLTR) builds and deploys the software platforms that act as the central operating system for its customers' data and decisions. Founded in 2003 by Peter Thiel, Alex Karp (CEO) and others, it began deep in the US defense/intelligence world and has, over two decades, become arguably the defining AI-software franchise of the West. Its products — Gotham (defense/intelligence), Foundry (commercial operations), Apollo (deployment infrastructure) and AIP (the Artificial Intelligence Platform, launched April 2023) — are all built around a distinctive Ontology: a live "digital twin" that maps an organisation's messy data to real-world objects, actions and decisions.

Two segments drive the business: Government (deeply entrenched defense/intelligence/civil-agency work) and Commercial (Fortune-500 enterprises adopting AIP). The investment story today is defined by a rare and stark tension. On one side, the business is exceptional and accelerating: Q2 2026 delivered 93% revenue growth (its fastest ever), 149% US-commercial growth, a Rule-of-40 score of 155%, 62% adjusted operating margins, and $1.2B of quarterly free cash flow — a combination of hyper-growth and deep profitability almost unheard-of at this scale. On the other side, the valuation is one of the most extreme in the entire market: ~40–70x sales and ~100x+ earnings, a level that discounts many years of continued hyper-growth to perfection and leaves little margin of safety. This report's central question is therefore not "is Palantir a great business?" (it clearly is) but "is any price too high, and how durable is the hyper-growth that price requires?"

Palantir is the market's ultimate "exceptional company, extraordinary valuation" battleground stock. That makes Palantir a growth-durability-versus-valuation bet, not a quality question, and it demands a valuation framework built around growth-adjusted multiples and Rule-of-40 durability rather than trailing earnings (see §15).

The Ontology is the heart of the moat — and the heart of the bear debate
Palantir's differentiator is the Ontology: rather than being a chatbot or a dashboard, it builds a living "digital twin" of an organisation that maps disparate, messy data to real-world objects (a specific aircraft, patient, shipment) and the actions that can be taken on them. AIP then layers large language models on top, so AI operates against a single, governed source of truth linked to reality — which is why Palantir claims its AI is more reliable and "action-ready" than generic tools. Real deployments cited include GE Aerospace (+26% production lift) and the US Navy's ShipOS (approval times cut from 200 hours to 15 seconds). The bull view: this integration is so deep and handles messy data so uniquely that it is a genuine, widening moat — "now everyone's copying" the ontology approach (Karp), validating the category. The bear view (Burry): the moat is really "obstruction of data transfer" — customers can't leave without losing years of analytical work, which is lock-in born of difficulty, not value, and "breeds resentment" in an era of data-portability and open architectures. Both can be partly true: the Ontology is a real, sticky advantage today, but whether it is a durable moat or an extraction-cost moat is exactly the uncertainty the valuation cannot afford to get wrong.

A real edge today — but the competitive question is the crux of the bear case
Palantir's competitive position is genuinely strong in the near term but hotly contested in the long term. Its edge is the Ontology-plus-action layer: where generic AI tools answer questions, Palantir claims to turn AI into governed, real-world action against a single source of truth — and it points to head-to-head "bake-off" wins over frontier labs (e.g., a lab failing a ticketing-automation problem that Palantir solved with "agent swarms," converting to a $10M contract) as proof. Forrester has ranked it a leader in AI/decisioning platforms. But three competitive threats define the bear case. (1) Frontier labs & plug-and-play AI (Anthropic, OpenAI): Burry and others argue that as models get more capable and "plug-and-play," the need to build an expensive ontology first may diminish — simpler tools could automate enterprise work without Palantir's data-infrastructure overhead. (2) Hyperscalers & data-platform giants (Microsoft, Snowflake, Databricks) bundling AI into existing enterprise footprints. (3) The "consulting vs software" critique: bears argue Palantir's forward-deployed-engineer model resembles high-touch consulting more than scalable software, which would cap long-run margins and multiples. The bull rebuttal is that broad adoption of ontology approaches validates the category Palantir pioneered, the TAM is vast enough for multiple winners, and the deepening integration is widening the moat. This unresolved competitive debate — more than anything operational — is what a 40–70x sales multiple is really betting on.

Cost Structure, Margins & Stock-Based Comp

Palantir's economics are now genuinely software-like: ~85% adjusted gross margins, 62% adjusted operating margin, and a 155% Rule of 40. But two cost items matter for a forensic read (see §14): stock-based compensation (SBC), which drives ongoing share dilution (~3.24% more shares YoY) and widens the gap between adjusted and GAAP profitability; and reliance on third-party LLMs and compute, a variable cost and strategic dependency (Q2 gross margin absorbed some hosting costs for one government customer).

Real profitability — but "adjusted" vs GAAP and dilution deserve scrutiny
The margin story is genuinely impressive and no longer in question: Palantir generates real GAAP operating income ($912M, 47% GAAP operating margin in Q2), >$1B GAAP net income run-rate, and $1.2B quarterly adjusted FCF — it has clearly crossed from "cash-burning growth story" to "profitable compounder." Two honest caveats for the careful analyst. (1) The adjusted-vs-GAAP gap is large — adjusted operating margin (62%) is well above GAAP (47%), and the difference is substantially stock-based compensation. SBC is a real economic cost to shareholders (it dilutes them), so headline "62% margins" and adjusted EPS flatter the picture versus GAAP. (2) Dilution is ongoing — shares outstanding rose ~3.24% over the year, a persistent headwind to per-share value that bears (Burry) highlight. Neither undermines the thesis that Palantir is now truly profitable — but both mean investors should anchor on GAAP earnings, free cash flow per share, and dilution-adjusted growth rather than the flattering adjusted headline numbers. The good news: FCF is large and growing fast enough that, if sustained, it increasingly covers SBC and supports buybacks over time.

Palantir's balance sheet is a genuine fortress: essentially no debt (D/E 0.02), a very high current ratio (~6.9x), a large cash/investments pile, and self-funding, rapidly-growing free cash flow (FY26 adj FCF guided $4.5–4.7B). A fortress balance sheet — which removes financial risk but not valuation risk
On balance-sheet quality, Palantir is about as strong as a company can be. The risk in Palantir isn't financial distress — it's that the price already embeds years of flawless hyper-growth. So investors should take real comfort in the balance sheet (it makes the business durable and the profitability high-quality) while recognising it is not a valuation cushion: a debt-free company can still fall 50% if growth decelerates from a 40–70x-sales starting point.

14

Forensic Analysis

Putting on the forensic hat: Palantir is a profitable, fortress-balance-sheet company — so this is not a distress or fraud-risk screen. It is instead the exact set of questions the bears (Michael Burry above all) have raised about earnings quality, dilution, receivables, revenue durability, and whether the reported profitability is as clean as the adjusted headlines suggest. The honest forensic conclusion up front: Palantir's accounting and cash generation are fundamentally sound — the real "forensic" issues are quality-of-earnings nuances (SBC/dilution, adjusted-vs-GAAP) and, above all, the fact that the valuation leaves no room for the normal imperfections these flags represent. Grade: "clean-and-cash-generative, but scrutinise the adjustments — and remember the price is the risk."

14.2 · Dilution & stock-based compensation

The most legitimate of the bear flags — a persistent per-share headwind
Dilution is the bear critique with the most straightforward validity. Palantir's share count rose ~3.24% over the past year, driven by heavy SBC. For a company this profitable it is manageable and shrinking as a percentage — and growing FCF increasingly funds buybacks that can offset it — but it is a real, recurring drag on per-share value that means per-share growth lags total growth, and it is a legitimate reason to prefer GAAP and per-share metrics. The mitigant: at $4.5–4.7B of FY26 FCF, Palantir has ample capacity to buy back stock and neutralise dilution over time if it chooses. Grade: amber — a genuine, quantifiable headwind, not a red flag, but one to track (watch share count and buyback pace each quarter).

14.3 · Revenue quality & receivables (Burry's specific charge)

Verify billings and DSO track revenue — the check bears demand
Burry's written critique specifically flags "ballooning accounts receivable" — the concern that revenue is being recognised faster than cash is collected, which can signal aggressive recognition or channel/deal-timing strain. This is the right forensic question to ask of any hyper-growth company signing large multi-year TCV deals (Q2 billings grew +88%, slightly below the +93% revenue growth — a small item worth noting). The strong counter-evidence: Palantir generated $1.22B of adjusted FCF in the quarter and guides to $4.5–4.7B for the year — enormous cash conversion that is very hard to fake and that directly rebuts a "revenue isn't turning into cash" thesis. A company converting profits to billions in real cash is not, on the whole, playing receivables games. The prudent forensic posture: monitor DSO, billings-vs-revenue, and deferred revenue/RPO trends quarter to quarter — if receivables consistently outgrow revenue and FCF weakens, that would be a genuine warning; today, robust FCF makes this an amber-to-watch, not a red flag.

14.4 · Cash-flow corroboration — the key positive

The strongest pillar of the forensic case
Cash-flow corroboration — usually the toughest forensic test — is Palantir's single strongest forensic positive. The company produced $1.22B of adjusted free cash flow in Q2 alone, crossed $1B milestones on GAAP net income, adjusted operating income and adjusted FCF simultaneously, and guides to $4.5–4.7B FCF for FY26. This is decisive because large, growing free cash flow is the hardest metric to manipulate — it validates that the reported profits are real, that revenue is converting to cash, and that the business model genuinely scales. It also underpins the fortress balance sheet and the capacity to offset dilution. Unlike a company whose "profits" never become cash (a classic red flag), Palantir's earnings are strongly cash-corroborated. Grade: green — this is what separates Palantir from a true "sandcastle"; the cash is unmistakably real.

14.5 · Business model — software or consulting?

The structural bear thesis — margins increasingly say "software"
A core Burry-style charge is that Palantir's forward-deployed-engineer (FDE) / boot-camp model resembles high-touch consulting more than scalable software — and if true, that would cap long-run margins and justify a far lower multiple (consulting firms trade at a fraction of software multiples). The forensic evidence increasingly cuts against the bear here: ~85% gross margins, 62% adjusted / 47% GAAP operating margins, a 155% Rule of 40, and revenue accelerating while margins expand are the signature of scaling software, not linear consulting — consulting cannot grow 93% while expanding margins, because it scales with headcount. The FDE model looks more like a high-touch land motion that then expands into high-margin platform consumption (land-and-expand). The residual caveat: the model is more services-intensive than pure SaaS, so investors should watch whether margin expansion and revenue-per-employee keep improving (they should, if it's truly software-like) rather than plateauing (which would validate the consulting critique). Grade: amber, trending favourable — the numbers look like software; keep verifying the operating leverage.

14.6 · Governance, insider selling & client-base risk

Normal-but-notable frictions — weigh them, don't overweight them
Three governance/conduct items belong in a forensic read. (1) Multi-class share structure concentrates control with the founders — standard for founder-led tech, but it limits external accountability. (2) Net insider selling of ~$562M over the trailing year: largely pre-planned 10b5-1 sales and founder diversification after an enormous run — not inherently a red flag, and common at richly-valued names — but persistent net selling by the best-informed insiders is a data point bears fairly cite at a perfection valuation. (3) Client-base/reputational risk: deep government work (DHS, Pentagon, IRS, ICE, ex-DOGE) carries genuine ESG/reputational considerations that have drawn public and even internal objection, and creates political/contract-replacement exposure. None of these is a smoking gun; collectively they are frictions to weigh in position-sizing rather than reasons to allege wrongdoing. Grade: amber — normal founder-tech governance frictions, monitored, not alarming.

14.7 · The real "forensic" flag — valuation risk

Where the genuine red flag actually sits
The honest forensic conclusion is that Palantir's biggest risk is not in its accounts — it's in its price. The financial statements are sound, the cash is real, the balance sheet is a fortress. But at ~40–70x sales and ~100–200x earnings, the stock embeds years of continued extraordinary hyper-growth with essentially no margin of safety. That is the true "red flag" a forensic-minded investor must foreground: not that the company will fail, but that the investment can lose heavily even if the business does very well — because merely "great" (rather than "otherworldly") results could trigger a severe de-rating from these multiples. Michael Burry's $46 fair value (a ~65–70% drawdown from current levels) and put options crystallise this; Simply Wall St's ~$90 fair value and GuruFocus's ~$144 GF Value sit far below the ~$160 price too. Bulls (Wedbush $230, Northland/DB $200) counter that growth this durable and category-defining deserves a premium multiple and that the guidance raise proves the runway. Both can be intellectually honest — which is precisely why this is a "valuation-versus-durability" bet. Grade: red on valuation risk — the dominant, defining risk of owning PLTR at these levels.
Forensic verdict
Palantir earns a "clean books, real cash, but the price is the risk" forensic grade. On the traditional forensic dimensions it is reassuring: genuinely GAAP-profitable, strongly cash-corroborated ($1.2B quarterly FCF is the opposite of a sandcastle), debt-free, and with accounting that is standard and disclosed rather than aggressive. The legitimate amber flags — SBC-driven dilution (~3.24%/yr), the adjusted-vs-GAAP gap, receivables/billings to monitor, the software-vs-consulting question, and founder-control/insider-selling — are real quality-of-earnings nuances worth tracking, but none suggests manipulation, and robust FCF rebuts the harshest ("sandcastle") charge. The one genuine red flag is not in the financials at all — it is the valuation: ~40–70x sales leaves no room for the ordinary imperfections these ambers represent, so the stock can fall hard on any deceleration even though the business is excellent. The forensic bottom line: believe the cash, respect the quality, insist on the GAAP lens — and treat the price, not the accounts, as the thing most likely to hurt you. Size accordingly.

Trailing P/E (~107–200x) is high but backward-looking and distorted by the recent profit inflection; the more useful lenses are forward EV/Sales, forward EV/FCF, growth-adjusted (PEG-style) multiples, and Rule-of-40 durability.
