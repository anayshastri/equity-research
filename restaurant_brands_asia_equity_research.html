01

Company Overview

Restaurant Brands Asia Ltd (RBA), formerly Burger King India Ltd, is the national master franchisee of the Burger King brand in India and the operator of Burger King and Popeyes in Indonesia — a listed quick-service-restaurant (QSR) company running two reportable segments (India and Indonesia). It is one of India's larger Western QSR chains by store count (~752 restaurants group-wide), competing with McDonald's (Westlife, Devyani), KFC/Pizza Hut (Sapphire/Devyani), Domino's (Jubilant) and a rising tide of local players and cloud kitchens.

The investment story today is defined by two things happening at once. First, a genuine operational turnaround in the core India business: Q1 FY27 delivered 12.6% same-store sales growth (SSSG) — the highest in 15 quarters — and India Company EBITDA (pre-Ind AS 116) surged +266% year-on-year, as tighter cost control, value offers and menu/format work finally translated scale into operating leverage. Second, a transformational change of ownership: in July 2026, Inspira Global (owner of homegrown QSR chain Chinese Wok) took a controlling ~42% stake (rising to ~48%) and injected ₹1,050 Cr of fresh capital, replacing the exiting private-equity owners (Everstone) with a food-service operator promising to accelerate growth and profitability. The catch: RBA is still loss-making at the net level (heavy depreciation and interest on a store-and-lease-heavy model, plus a persistent Indonesia drag), so this is a revenue-compounding, profitability-still-elusive turnaround with fresh capital and a new driver — not yet an earnings story.

The investment character in one line
RBA is a loss-making QSR growth-and-turnaround play at an inflection: a fast-scaling, momentum-rich India Burger King business (record SSSG, EBITDA inflecting) that has just been recapitalised and handed to a new, food-service-savvy, capital-rich promoter — but which still bleeds at the net line thanks to depreciation, interest and a loss-making Indonesia arm. The bull case is a classic QSR operating-leverage + new-owner turnaround: revenue compounds, SSSG stays strong, store economics improve, Indonesia is fixed or exited, and the business finally crosses into profit — re-rating on the promise. The bear case is that it stays loss-making longer than hoped, Indonesia keeps draining cash, competition/discounting pressures margins, and a ~6x-book valuation on a loss-maker proves too rich. This is an EV/sales-and-turnaround-execution bet, not an earnings-multiple investment — value it on the path to profitability, not trailing P/E.

History & Evolution

2013 · Burger King enters India
Burger King India Ltd formed as the national master franchisee, backed by Everstone Capital (via QSR Asia); opens its first Indian restaurants and scales rapidly through the late 2010s.
Dec 2020 · Blockbuster IPO
Burger King India lists in a hugely-oversubscribed IPO, riding QSR-growth enthusiasm — one of the most talked-about listings of the year.
2022 · Indonesia acquisition & rename
Acquires the Burger King & Popeyes Indonesia business and renames itself Restaurant Brands Asia — a bid to become a multi-country QSR platform. Indonesia later becomes the problem child.
FY23–FY25 · Growth without profit
Aggressive India store expansion drives revenue, but persistent net losses (depreciation, interest, Indonesia drag) and weak SSSG in a tough demand environment leave the stock down heavily (−55% over five years).
Jan–Jul 2026 · The Inspira takeover
Inspira Global agrees (Jan) and completes (Jul) a change of control at ₹70/share: buys out old promoters, infuses ₹1,050 Cr, launches an open offer; Everstone exits. Lenexis Foodworks becomes promoter (~42%→48%).
Aug 2026 · Turnaround shows up in the numbers
Q1 FY27: India SSSG 12.6% (15-quarter high), EBITDA +266%, losses narrowing — the stock hits a 20% upper circuit. The new owner inherits genuine momentum.

RBA's value is overwhelmingly the India Burger King business (83% of revenue) — a large, scaling, momentum-rich chain that is the reason to own the stock and the part that's clearly working. The India Burger King business is firing on all cylinders: Q1 FY27 revenue up 23.6% to ₹682.8 Cr, SSSG of 12.6% — the best in 15 quarters, driven by strong dine-in and delivery traction and sharp value offerings (₹79 two-burger deals, ₹99 meals). Full-year FY26 India revenue rose 15.4% to ₹2,271.7 Cr with Company EBITDA (pre-Ind AS 116) up 33.2%. The chain is expanding (~80 new stores planned in FY27) while finally showing operating leverage.

Why the India momentum is credible — and what's driving it
This is the strongest part of the thesis, and the momentum looks real rather than cosmetic. Industry-leading SSSG of 12.6% (accelerating from ~2.8% the prior quarter and 4% for FY26) reflects a genuine demand recovery in Indian QSR (moderating inflation, consumption-tax cuts leaving consumers with more to spend) that RBA is capturing through aggressive value menus, dual-channel strength (dine-in + delivery), and disciplined cost control. Crucially, this SSSG is flowing through to operating leverage — a 266% jump in India Company EBITDA shows that as same-store sales climb on a largely-fixed store cost base, incremental revenue drops disproportionately to operating profit. That is exactly the dynamic a QSR turnaround needs: a maturing store base + rising SSSG + new-store additions = compounding operating profit. The risks to watch: the value-led SSSG is partly discount-driven (which can pressure per-unit margins), and Western QSR faces intense competition from local diners and cloud kitchens. But the direction — strong, accelerating, leverage-generating India growth — is the clearest positive in the whole story.

Q1 FY27 was a genuine operating inflection, and the market rewarded it with a 20% upper circuit. Revenue grew 18%, India SSSG hit a 15-quarter high, and — the key number — Company EBITDA (pre-Ind AS 116) leapt 266% to ₹43.5 Cr, with margin expanding from 1.7% to 5.3%. Two clarifications matter. So the honest read is strongly improving, clearly inflecting — but not yet profitable. The trajectory is what matters: if SSSG and operating leverage hold, the pre-116 EBITDA line is where profitability will first become visible, quarter by quarter.

The July 2026 change of control brought in Inspira Global — owner of homegrown QSR brands including Chinese Wok (250+ restaurants across 45+ cities, 10+ years of QSR operating experience) — as the new promoter via Lenexis Foodworks (Aayush Madhusudan Agrawal). Inspira paid ₹70/share (~10% premium), bought out the old PE owners, and infused ₹1,050 Cr of fresh capital (with ₹450 Cr more on warrant exercise), taking a controlling ~42% (→~48%) stake. Everstone Capital fully exited.

A strategic operator, not a financial owner — the potential upgrade, and the leverage caveat
This is potentially a meaningful upgrade in ownership character. RBA moves from a private-equity owner (Everstone), whose horizon is an exit, to a strategic food-service operator (Inspira/Chinese Wok) whose expertise is running QSR chains. The thesis: Inspira brings operational know-how (supply chain, unit economics, menu, delivery), a long-term orientation, and — critically — ₹1,050 Cr of fresh capital to accelerate India expansion, fix or rationalise Indonesia, and invest in digital/brand. For a business that had been cash-tightening (FY26 closing cash fell to ₹30 Cr from ₹534 Cr as capex outran operating cash), that recapitalisation is genuinely de-risking. The caveats to weigh: (1) execution is unproven at this scale — running a ~750-store, two-country, multi-brand platform is a big step up from Chinese Wok; (2) there is an LBO feature — Lenexis pledged ~16.7% of the company's shares (~₹844 Cr) to fund the acquisition, so the new promoter itself carries acquisition leverage (a risk if the stock or business weakens); and (3) minority investors are, in effect, underwriting a new owner's turnaround plan. On balance, a capital-rich strategic operator taking charge of an inflecting business is a positive setup — but it is a promise to be proven, with a leverage asterisk.

Indonesia (17% of revenue) is the drag on the story. The segment's FY26 revenue fell (to ₹550.9 Cr from ₹582.9 Cr) and RBA took a ₹120 Cr impairment on its Indonesia subsidiary. Within it, Burger King Indonesia is improving — it reached positive store-level EBITDA in Q4 FY26 after closing underperforming stores — but Popeyes Indonesia keeps losing money (negative store EBITDA). The key swing factor the new owner must resolve — fix or exit
Indonesia is where the bear case lives and where the new promoter's first big strategic decision sits. The good news is directional improvement — BK Indonesia turning store-EBITDA-positive after rationalisation shows the unit economics can work with discipline. The bad news is that the segment as a whole still loses money, shrank in FY26, and forced a ₹120 Cr writedown — a persistent cash and earnings drain on an already loss-making group. For minority investors, the cleanest bull outcome is a decisive resolution: either turn Indonesia (especially Popeyes) to sustained profit quickly, or scale it back / exit it to stop the bleed and let the profitable, high-momentum India business carry the valuation. Continued muddle — a slowly-improving-but-still-loss-making Indonesia — is the outcome that keeps consolidated profitability out of reach and the stock's loss-making optics intact. How Inspira handles Indonesia is one of the two or three most important things to watch, alongside India SSSG and the path to consolidated PAT breakeven.

The QSR model's economics are the crux of the loss: positive operating cash flow (₹302 Cr in FY26) shows the stores generate cash operationally, but heavy depreciation and interest (owned/leased store assets, debt) push the accounting result into loss, and aggressive expansion capex (₹485 Cr) outran operating cash, so closing cash fell dramatically to ₹30 Cr from ₹534 Cr.

The recapitalisation is the point — it converts a cash-squeeze into a growth runway
This is exactly why the ₹1,050 Cr infusion matters so much. Entering FY27, RBA was in a cash-tightening squeeze — operating cash was positive but insufficient to fund both aggressive expansion and its loss-making Indonesia arm, and the cash balance had collapsed to ₹30 Cr, raising real questions about future funding. The new promoter's fresh capital resolves that at a stroke, giving RBA the balance sheet to keep expanding India (80 stores/year), invest in digital and brand, and absorb the Indonesia turnaround without a liquidity scare. The deeper point for valuation: because the business is operationally cash-generative (positive OCF) and the losses are largely non-cash depreciation + interest + growth investment, the path to reported profitability runs through operating leverage (rising SSSG on a maturing store base) eventually outgrowing the D&A/interest load — which is precisely the dynamic Q1 FY27 began to show. Watch the OCF-to-capex balance, the cash position post-infusion, and the point at which EBITDA growth covers the fixed financing/depreciation load.

Note the data quirk: some screeners still show "promoter 9.22%" — a mid-transition reclassification artifact, not the real control position.

A strategic long-term owner — but with an acquisition-leverage flag
The governance picture has genuine positives and one clear flag. Positive: control has passed to a strategic food-service operator with a long-term orientation and fresh capital, replacing a finite-horizon PE owner — generally a better alignment for a turnaround that needs patient investment. The flag: Lenexis funded part of the acquisition with leverage, pledging ~16.7% of the company's shares (~₹844 Cr) against the purchase. Promoter share pledges are a well-known governance risk — if the stock falls sharply, pledge top-ups or forced selling can amplify downside, and it signals the new owner is stretched rather than buying with pure equity. Net: a promising ownership upgrade in character (strategic vs financial), with a leverage caveat that tempers it — and, given the recency, a governance situation still settling into its new shape.

The balance sheet is transformed by the recapitalisation — ₹1,050 Cr in (₹450 Cr more to come) refills a depleted cash position and funds growth. But the starting point is weak: years of losses have eroded book value (hence the optically high ~5.7–7x P/B), 3-year ROE is deeply negative (~−27%), and interest coverage is low (screener-flagged) given the debt and lease load.

The metric that decides the thesis: the path to consolidated PAT breakeven
For a loss-making QSR turnaround, conventional balance-sheet ratios (P/B, ROE) are distorted and not the right anchor — book is eroded by cumulative losses (making P/B look high) and ROE is negative by definition while the company loses money. The metric that actually decides the investment is the credible path to consolidated PAT breakeven and beyond: when does rising India EBITDA (from SSSG + operating leverage + new stores), plus an Indonesia fix/exit, finally outgrow the depreciation + interest load to produce sustained net profit? The building blocks are visibly improving — pre-Ind AS 116 EBITDA inflecting (+266% in Q1), positive operating cash flow, fresh capital reducing funding risk, and India momentum strong. But the market is paying ~6x book and an EV that implies profitability is coming — so the whole thesis rests on that path being real and near. Track: pre-Ind AS 116 EBITDA margin trajectory, India SSSG durability, Indonesia's drag, the D&A/interest load vs EBITDA, and each step toward the first profitable quarters. Breakeven timing is the number that matters.

Bottom line
Restaurant Brands Asia is a loss-making QSR growth-and-turnaround at a genuine, early inflection. Its core India Burger King business is firing — Q1 FY27 delivered 12.6% same-store growth (a 15-quarter high) and a 266% jump in pre-Ind AS 116 EBITDA, as demand recovery, value menus and operating leverage finally converted scale into rising operating profit — and it has just been recapitalised (₹1,050 Cr) and handed to a strategic, food-service-experienced new promoter, Inspira Global (Chinese Wok), replacing exiting private-equity owners. That combination — strong, accelerating India momentum + fresh capital + an operator-owner — is a genuinely attractive turnaround setup. But the honest caveats are real: RBA is still loss-making at the net line (FY26 −₹204 Cr including a ₹120 Cr Indonesia impairment; Q1 −₹28 Cr), the Indonesia arm (especially Popeyes) keeps draining cash, competition and discounting are brutal, the new promoter funded part of its buyout with a ~₹844 Cr share pledge, execution at this scale is unproven, and the stock trades at ~5.7–7x an eroded book — pricing in the profitable future rather than the loss-making present. The framing: a credible, capital-backed QSR turnaround whose India engine is inflecting impressively and whose new owner brings money and expertise — but which must still cross into sustained profitability, fix or exit Indonesia, and prove the plan, all while valued for success. Judge it on EV/Sales, pre-Ind AS 116 EV/EBITDA, SSSG and the path to breakeven — never trailing P/E or P/B — make new-promoter execution the re-rating gate and Indonesia the swing factor, and own it only as the high-beta turnaround bet it is. The momentum and the recapitalisation are real; the profits, and the proof, are still ahead.

The metric that decides the thesis: EBITDA outgrowing the D&A + interest load
RBA's losses are fundamentally about a store-and-lease-heavy cost structure: the business makes operating cash, but depreciation on store assets and interest on debt/leases sit above the net line and turn it red. The decisive question is therefore when rising EBITDA — powered by SSSG, operating leverage and new-store maturation — durably outgrows that fixed D&A + interest load (and Indonesia stops subtracting), producing sustained net profit. Q1 FY27's 266% pre-Ind AS 116 EBITDA jump is the first strong evidence the gap is closing; the fresh capital buys the time and funds the expansion to keep closing it. Watch: pre-Ind AS 116 EBITDA margin trend, the absolute EBITDA-vs-(D&A+interest) gap each quarter, the post-infusion cash position and pledge level, and Indonesia's drag. The quarter the group crosses into sustained profitability is the re-rating trigger — and the balance-sheet strength (now restored) is what ensures it can get there without a funding scare.
